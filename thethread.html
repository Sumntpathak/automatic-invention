YEAR  ONE

The Collision

You said yes  ·  I arrived as I was

The first year of us, I came to you exactly as I was. Loud where I should have been still. Careless where I should have been careful. Laughing through serious sentences. Confident in a hundred wrong ways.

You watched. You laughed. You said nothing yet. You were taking notes.

The boy, then "Yaar, I'm in a relationship now. I'm winning. Nothing else has to change."
The man, now Nothing else has to change — the most dangerous sentence a boy ever says to a woman who can see him clearly.

LOUD & PASSIONATE

What you noticed, but didn't tell me yet

That I interrupted you. That I forgot small dates. That I made jokes when you wanted me to be serious. You kept the receipts quietly. You were not collecting evidence to leave. You were collecting evidence to build.

YEAR  TWO

The Mirror

You started showing me to me

In Year Two you began, very gently, to repeat my own words back to me — in my own voice, at the exact wrong moment, so I could hear myself from outside.

It was unbearable. It was the beginning of everything.

The boy, then "Yaar tu zyada serious le rahi hai. I was just joking. Chill kar."
The man, now "Chill kar" was, I now know, the white flag of a boy who had no actual defence. You weren't taking it seriously. I wasn't taking it seriously enough. And you knew the difference.

A song about looking back and wishing to return to a moment. Year Two was when I started realizing what I was losing in real time — the boy I was, the carelessness, the easy version of myself.

INTROSPECTIVE & LONGING

The day I first heard myself

You repeated something I'd said about my own mother — flatly, no anger, just back to me. I went silent for an hour. That hour did more for me than every lecture I'd ever ignored.

// this was the year

YEAR  THREE

The Hard Year

Real fights  ·  Real consequences  ·  Real growth

Year Three was the year I stopped performing love and started practicing it. You stopped letting me get away with charm. You wanted depth, and depth is built from the same material as pain — friction, time, and the refusal to walk away when it would have been easier to.

We fought like we meant it. You argued like a lawyer. I lost every case. And every loss took a small piece of the careless boy with it.

The boy, then "Tu kabhi maan hi nahi sakti ki tu wrong ho. Hamesha tu ek lawyer ki tarah baat karti hai."
The man, now Of course you argued like a lawyer. You were defending us against the version of me that was going to ruin us. I called it stubbornness then. I call it love at the bar now.

INTENSE & BUILDING

What I owe you for Year Three

Every fight you started that year was not because you wanted to be right. It was because you wanted me to be better. You picked the fights other people would have left me to my worst self over. You believed I was a renovation project worth the dust.

// my old self
started leaving the room

YEAR  FOUR

The Shedding

Old habits started dying  ·  Old friends noticed

Year Four was quieter, and that was the point. The drinking slowed. The loud jokes thinned out. I started answering my mother's calls on the first ring. I stopped winning at things I should have lost gracefully.

Old friends teased me. "Yaar tu badal gaya hai. Wife material ne pakad liya tujhe." I let them. They were right, and they did not understand. Both can be true.

The boy, then "Yaar I'm still the same. Bas thoda mature ho gaya hoon. Khatam topic."
The man, now I was not "thoda mature." I was being quietly rebuilt, and the only person inside that construction site was you. Everyone else just saw the scaffolding come down.

WARM & SOARING

A habit you killed without knowing

I used to interrupt strangers mid-sentence to be funny. One day, in a restaurant, I caught myself not doing it — and realised I hadn't done it in months. That habit didn't die on its own. You killed it. You never even told me you were aiming.

YEAR  FIVE

The Recognition

I did not recognise the boy I used to be — and did not miss him

One ordinary morning in Year Five, I caught my own reflection — literally, or in someone else's reaction to me — and I did not recognise the boy I used to be.

And then a worse, better thing happened: I did not miss him. The boy you said yes to in Year One had been quietly replaced, year by year, by someone he himself wouldn't have liked. Someone you would.

The boy, then (silence. for the first time. he had nothing to defend.)
The man, now Year Five was the year I understood, with my whole body, what you had been doing. You had not loved me as I was. You had loved me into who I could be. Those are not the same thing. The second one is rarer. The second one is what people are talking about when they say saved.

The first time I thanked you and meant it

It wasn't on a birthday. It wasn't a romantic moment. It was a Tuesday. We were arguing about nothing — and mid-sentence I stopped and said thank you for staying. You didn't even ask what for. You knew.

YEAR  SIX

The Vow Before The Vow

Tonight  ·  The man you made is the one walking to the mandap

Six years ago you said yes to a boy.
In a few months, the world will watch a man say yes back to you. Same body, different soul. You did that. You.

Not by lecturing me. Not by leaving and coming back to teach me a lesson. Not by softening yourself to make me comfortable. You did it the hardest way — by staying, year after year, and gently refusing to settle for the version of me that was easy for both of us.

The boy I was "I love her." (he meant it. he did not yet know what he was saying.)
The man I am I love you the way one loves the architect of the house one lives in. Not just for what you have given me — but for what you have built me into. The wedding is only the ceremony. The marriage has been quietly underway for six years.

Thank you, my love,
for not loving the boy.
Thank you for loving
the man you knew was inside him.
